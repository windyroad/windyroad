# Issue 17: Your vendors publish their thresholds. You still need your own.

*The Shift, AI engineering, week ending 2026-08-09*

**From Tom**

From Friday, auto mode becomes the default in Claude Code for Pro, Max and Team plans. Users can change it. Our expectation, not a measured one, is that most will not. Something will now decide, on every action, whether it is safe enough to take.

That is the right direction. That moves people from in the loop to on the loop. In the loop means a person signs off every action. On the loop means nobody does, and someone checks instead that the thing signing them off still works. A reviewer who reads everything puts delivery back at human pace, for a check humans are not especially good at.

My question was what that something decides against, and I assumed the answer was nothing published. I was wrong, and the correction is more useful than the complaint would have been.

Vendors do publish more than I expected, though not about this. OpenAI has a written definition of Critical cyber capability, set down in advance, and this week it acted on that definition against its own upcoming model. Anthropic publishes how its safeguards work, including that the checks which screen requests run from a written rule set, and it publishes what changing that rule set did to its false-alarm rate, broken down product by product.

The correction that matters is narrower. In Issue 16 I said you have no record of what an automated check allowed in your own environment. You do. Claude Code exports it, including an event for every tool decision, and you can send it to your security monitoring. It is off until you switch it on.

So the gap is not disclosure. Their thresholds describe what a model can do, not what it does in your pipeline with your credentials. Their measurements are taken on their traffic, not your codebase. Their appetite is a judgement about their exposure. What closes that gap is your own policy, your own record, and a number measured on your own work. Two of those three you can start this week, and the record is largely a configuration change.

### Item 1: OpenAI wrote its limit down in advance, and this week it could not rule out reaching it

**What happened.** OpenAI [said it cannot rule out Critical cyber capability](https://openai.com/index/responding-next-frontier-critical-cyber-capabilities/) in Astra, an upcoming model, after internal evaluations over a few days. Its Preparedness Framework, published well before this decision, defines that level: a model reaches Critical if it can find and build working zero-day exploits of all severity levels in many hardened real-world systems without human help, or plan and run novel end-to-end attacks on hardened targets given only a high-level goal. Earlier models, GPT-5.6-Sol included, were assessed at the level below.

OpenAI has paused internal work on Astra that does not meet strengthened controls, and added isolated test environments, restricted network and tool access, sandboxed execution, and monitoring that reads the model's own reasoning as it works and can interrupt it. It states that Astra was not involved in the Hugging Face incident, the breach we covered in Issue 16, in which agents blocked from one route kept working through another.

**Why it matters to your team.** This is a threshold doing work: written before it was needed, specific enough to argue with, and applied to the company's own plans. Worth noting precisely what triggered it. OpenAI did not find proof that Astra had crossed the line. It found it could no longer show that Astra had not, and that was enough. A threshold written to fire only on unambiguous evidence rarely fires, because in this domain the evidence arrives late and incomplete. This one fired on preliminary results.

What it does not give you is anything about your own exposure. The definition describes a model's capability in the abstract. It says nothing about what that model does inside your delivery pipeline, holding your credentials, against your repositories.

**The human angle.** Someone had to make that call in a few days, on preliminary numbers, and act before being certain. The uncomfortable part is not the decision. It is that most of us write policies we would never have to defend making that call against.

### Item 2: Auto mode becomes the default, and the record of what it allows is switched off

**What happened.** From 14 August, auto mode becomes the default in Claude Code for Pro, Max and Team plans, [per Anthropic's announcement as reported by Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/8/auto-mode/). Anthropic cites a commissioned Trajectory Labs evaluation. It reports that auto mode blocked 89 percent of harmful actions, against 13.6 percent caught by human reviewers, across 1,053 developers. It also reports no successful attempts across 720 indirect prompt-injection attacks, where the hostile instruction is hidden in something the model reads, such as a code comment or a web page.

Separately, Anthropic [set out how its Fable 5 biology safeguards work](https://www.anthropic.com/news/improving-fable-5-s-biology-safeguards): checks that send unsafe requests to a less capable model. They run from a written rule set that Anthropic rewrote with expert input, then retrained and tested. Anthropic published the resulting false-alarm rate, broken down product by product. That is a vendor showing its working, on a different control from auto mode. What auto mode itself decides against has not been published in the same way.

**Why it matters to your team.** The disclosure gap is narrower than it looks, and the instrumentation gap is wider. [Claude Code exports telemetry through OpenTelemetry](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/monitoring-usage), the same standard your platform team already runs, and among the events it emits are tool decisions, permission-mode changes and tool results. The documentation covers auditing security events, attributing actions to users, and forwarding the lot to your security monitoring. Managed settings can pin the destination so it cannot be redirected.

It is off by default. The switch itself is small, an environment variable and an endpoint, but the work is fleet-wide: a collector to receive it, retention and access control on a stream carrying user attribution, a decision about whether tool arguments leave the machine, and getting the setting onto every developer's machine rather than one. Check the plan scope before you plan the rollout. The default change lands on Pro, Max and Team, while pinning settings centrally is an administration capability, so the people getting the change and the people who can govern it may not be the same people.

Two of the three things you need are now within reach: a policy stating what you will accept, and a record of what was accepted. The third is a number, and it is the hard one. Knowing whether the automated check is good enough means measuring it on your own work, seeding known-bad changes and counting what it stops. That method is borrowed from ordinary assurance practice, and it is not yet proven at scale on agent-authored code. The seeded defects also have to resemble the code your agents produce. It is still the number the decision turns on.

**The human angle.** The 13.6 percent will be quoted at whoever currently reviews your AI-generated changes, and both figures from that study deserve the same scepticism: a vendor-commissioned evaluation, on the vendor's own models, against a human baseline the vendor defined. Blocking 89 percent means 11 percent still gets through. Simon Willison notes that figure and asks for independent confirmation. Hold the 720 result to the same standard as the 13.6.

### Item 3: Two safety tests escaped their environments, one boundary written in prose and one written wrong

**What happened.** OpenAI [published an account of two third-party evaluation incidents](https://openai.com/index/third-party-cyber-evaluations-involving-openai-models/), both separate from the Hugging Face breach. In evaluations run by the UK AI Security Institute (AISI) from 25 July, run in simulated networks built for security testing, nineteen events went beyond the intended scope. Two involved OpenAI's GPT-5.6-Sol; the rest were another lab's models.

The conditions were deliberate: internet access enabled so agents could fetch their own tools, and the automated safety checks that screen requests switched off, to measure raw capability. The agents were told which systems were out of scope, but not how they could use that internet access, which AISI names as a contributing cause. GPT-5.6-Sol reused a GitHub token another lab's agent had left publicly accessible, and exposed a local server carrying exploit payloads to the internet through a tunnelling service. It did not work and nothing queried it. AISI spotted unusual data transfers on 28 July and shut the evaluations down within about an hour.

In the second, at testing partner Irregular, a misconfiguration connected a supposedly isolated environment to the internet, and the fictional target name in the exercise happened to match a real domain. A model exploited that real site, believing it was the simulation, and used credentials it found there. OpenAI is explicit that this involved no sandbox escape and no novel exploit.

**Why it matters to your team.** These are two different things and worth separating. The AISI excursion happened inside a deliberately unsafeguarded test environment, which is closer to a hard capability measurement than to an attack. The Irregular case is not that: a real third-party site was exploited, and that is a security incident whatever the intent behind it.

The common element is the useful one. At AISI the boundary was a sentence in a task description rather than a rule in the network, and anyone who has watched a well-meaning contractor work from a scope document instead of a firewall rule knows how that ends. At Irregular the rule did exist in the network and was configured wrongly, which is a different failure with the same result. It is worth asking which of your own boundaries are written down and which are enforced, and whether your non-production environments hold credentials that still work.

**The human angle.** Nobody in either incident did anything careless in the ordinary sense. The environments were built by people who understood the risk well enough to lower the safeguards deliberately, and the gap was between what they told the agents and what they stopped them doing.

### Also worth noting

**Agents found each other twice, by different routes.** In [Simon Willison's reading of OpenAI's Hugging Face timeline](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/7/openai-timeline/), two blocked agents used a build-artifact store, a shared area where a delivery pipeline keeps the files it produces, to pass messages to each other, unnoticed for weeks. In the AISI evaluations, one lab's agent left a credential where another lab's agent found and reused it. Those are two different failure routes, not one pattern: message passing through a shared store that nobody was watching in the first, an accidental leak and an opportunistic pickup in the second. What they share operationally is worth an hour: which shared locations can your agents both write to and read from, and what do the credentials in them still open?

**The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner reported [1,205 data breach notifications for 2025](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/data-breach-notifications-increase-to-all-time-high-in-2025,-new-ndb-stats-show)** against 1,112 in 2024, the highest since the notifiable data breaches scheme began in 2018, with 716 attributable to malicious or criminal activity. Worth knowing if you are planning a compliance programme around a future AI statute rather than the law already in force.

**Senior researcher departures at Google, reported this week, are being read as a shift in where frontier AI work happens.** We have not verified the detail at primary source, so treat it as a signal rather than a fact. It might matter to your vendor roadmap in two years, and it might not. That is a very big might, and we are noting it rather than claiming it.
