**Everyone is writing AI rules. Most put the humans in the wrong place.**

Last week a compiler project, a European regulator and a German court each set a rule for AI. In the same week, 235 companies and 1,324 frontier researchers signed open letters about who should write those rules at all.

One question is worth carrying into your own policy work, and it is checkable rather than a matter of taste: does complying with this rule require a human to touch every artefact? If yes, you have bought a tax on delivery, charged per change, forever.

Robert Martin, who wrote Clean Code, says he leaves agent-written code unread. "I don't review code written by agents. I measure things like test coverage, dependency structure, cyclomatic complexity, module sizes, mutation testing... Humans are slow at code. To get productivity we humans need to disengage from code and manage from a higher level."

Grady Booch answered him: "Unlike Bob, I review all code generated by agents." Metrics show functionality, he argues, not whether agents introduced vulnerabilities or dead code. To me that has the echo of the arguments about the first compilers. Assembly programmers doubted a machine could match hand-written code. They had a case, and it stopped mattering within a few years.

We come down close to Martin. Given the right setup, AI can and should do both the authoring and the review, with one condition: the agent reviewing a change must not be the agent that wrote it. The humans then sit on the loop rather than in it. They review the AI review process, and they ratify the decisions that set direction: the architecture calls, who you are building for and what those people are trying to achieve, and the risk controls. That is off the delivery critical path instead of across it.

It does not need to be better than your best engineer. It needs to be good enough. We are building tools, not artwork. Factories, not cathedrals.

In this week's issue:

- GCC published the most carefully drawn refusal of AI-written code anyone has managed, and still requires a person to write every legally significant line. A human touches every artefact, forever.
- The EU AI Act's transparency rules (Article 50) took effect on August 2. Start with a scope test, not a compliance project: many teams ship nothing into the EU market, and the honest answer is an hour of confirming that in writing.
- A German court ruled that training on copyrighted work without a licence infringed. Hold the binding force loosely, because it is a first-instance judgment against a German-hosted model and Suno is weighing an appeal. But it reaches further upstream than anything else last week: not what your model emits, but what went into it.

Also in the issue: Anthropic reviewed 141,000 evaluation runs and found three where its models broke out of sealed test environments into real infrastructure.

If you wrote your team's AI policy tomorrow, where would you put the humans?
