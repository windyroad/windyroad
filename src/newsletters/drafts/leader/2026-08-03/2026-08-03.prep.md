# Issue 16: Everyone wrote AI rules this week. Most wrote the wrong one.

*The Shift, AI engineering, week ending 2026-08-02*

**From Tom**

I read five attempts to set AI rules this week, and reading them together gave me a test for which ones are worth copying. It is a single question: does the rule govern the system that produces the work, or does it govern the work itself?

To keep myself honest, the unit is fixed: the work is the thing that ships, and the system is the pipeline that produces and checks it. A rule that says something about what may ship is an output rule. A rule that says something about how the pipeline must be built or run is a system rule.

That test matters because of where it leaves the people. Govern the output and you end up with humans inspecting every change, which puts your delivery back at human speed, the thing you adopted the tools to escape. Govern the system and you can put people where they compound: on the gates, on the tests that grade what the model produces, and on the architecture calls. Machines on the code, people on the system that judges the code. Call it the arrangement.

Five is not everything that happened, it is everything this week that tried to set a rule about AI development itself. Australia's privacy regulator also published guidance that bites today, and it scores as a system rule; it is in Also worth noting because it governs a specific technology rather than AI development at large.

Here is the scorecard. Two govern the system. Two govern the output. One governs neither.

1. **GCC**, the compiler project underpinning much of the Linux world, banned AI from writing code but left it free to review. Letting the machine review is the half it got right. Forcing people to write every line is an output rule, and we think it is the half that will not hold.
2. **The EU's transparency deadline** took effect. It governs what you tell users about the output, not who checks the system that produced it. A labelling rule.
3. **More than a thousand frontier researchers** asked the US government to help pace automated AI development. A rule about speed, which is neither.
4. **Anthropic's counter-proposal**, that every sufficiently capable model face mandatory pre-release safety testing, open or closed, governs what has to happen before a system ships. System rule.
5. **A bipartisan bill** requiring developers to keep the technical ability to stop their own models governs a property the system must have. Also a system rule. It is covered under Also worth noting, below.

Only two of those five bind anyone today: GCC's policy and the EU deadline. The other three are a position paper, an open letter and an unpassed bill. Which is worth holding onto, because the ones that bind are both output rules, and the ones that got the shape right are the ones nobody has to follow yet.

### Item 1: GCC, the compiler behind most Linux software, banned AI from writing code and left it free to review

**What happened:**
  - The GCC steering committee [published an AI policy](https://lwn.net/Articles/1086041/) on July 29 governing contributions written with LLMs (large language models). It declines any "legally significant contributions which include LLM-generated content or are derived from LLM-generated content," with legally significant set at around 15 lines of code or text, following existing GNU maintainer guidelines. There is a narrow exception for test cases.
  - The same policy explicitly permits using a model for research, analysis, bug discovery and reporting, and patch review, as long as none of that output reaches the contribution itself.
  - The committee says it "expects the policy will evolve and will be revisited periodically."

**Why it matters to your team:** GCC is the latest major open-source project to formally refuse AI-authored contributions, and it is the most carefully drawn refusal we have seen. It gets half the arrangement right. Letting a language model hunt bugs and review patches is the half we have argued for since Issue 06, and it satisfies the one condition we attached in Issue 14, that the agent reviewing a change must not be the agent that wrote it, because under this policy no agent wrote it. The problem is the other half. GCC reserved the writing, the part that scales, for people. Its stated reason is legal provenance rather than code quality. That is a real concern, GCC's maintainers carry it for decades, and we are not going to second-guess a volunteer project's read of its own legal exposure.

The part worth arguing with is the other half: reserving the writing for people. That is a rule about who produces the output, and it is the half that pins the project to human speed on the one activity where the machine is now demonstrably fast. If you are drafting your own AI contribution policy this quarter, that is the trap to avoid: a number you can count is not the same as the thing you are worried about. The more durable question is not how much AI-written code you will tolerate. It is who checks that your review process still catches what it is supposed to catch, and how often you test that it does. Google's Chrome result below is what that looks like when it works.

**The human angle:** GCC's maintainers are volunteers who carry the maintenance cost of every patch for years, and their stated rationale is legal provenance rather than code quality. That is a real constraint and worth respecting. It is also a reminder that a policy can be entirely rational for the project that wrote it and still be the wrong template for yours, which is the trap in copying one.

### Item 2: The EU's AI transparency rule took effect on August 2

**What happened:**
  - The EU AI Act's [Article 50 transparency obligations](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/transparency-rules-article-50/) took legal effect on August 2. Providers and deployers placing AI systems on the EU market must tell people when they are interacting with AI, and mark AI-generated content.
  - We flagged this as six days out in Issue 15. It is now in force.
  - In Issue 15 we also pointed at the AI Omnibus package, which would move the machine-readable marking requirement to December 2. The December 2 date applies to generative AI systems already on the EU market before August 2, which get until then to bring machine-readable marking into conformity. It rests on a provisional agreement rather than adopted text, so the guidance stands unchanged. Plan against August 2 and treat December 2 as relief not yet granted. The European Commission's [transparency-obligations FAQ](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act) is the authoritative source.

**Why it matters to your team:** Start with a scope test, not a compliance project. Article 50 binds systems placed on the EU market, and a large share of the teams reading this ship nothing into it. If that is you, the work is an hour spent confirming it, in writing, so the question stops recurring. If you do reach EU users, including through a customer's product or a self-serve signup you never targeted, the obligation is narrower than the surrounding noise suggests: people have to be told when they are dealing with AI or with AI-generated content. List the features you ship that surface either, and confirm each one tells the user. Unlike most of what we have tracked for a year, this one arrived on its date and was not reversed, which is why it changes planning when a run of bills did not. Worth naming what it does not do, though. Article 50 governs what you tell users about the output. It says nothing about who checks the system that produced it, which is the question that determines whether the output is any good. It is a labelling rule, and a labelling rule is cheap to satisfy and easy to satisfy badly.

**The human angle:** Whoever owns compliance in your organisation has spent two years being told a deadline was coming. The unglamorous version of a good week for them is being handed a one-page answer to "does this apply to us" that an engineer wrote and can defend, rather than being left to derive it from the regulation.

### Item 3: The industry wrote its own AI rules, and mostly aimed at the wrong target

**What happened:**
  - A Microsoft-led open letter, ["Open Weights and American AI Leadership"](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/2/open-letters/), carried 235 company signatures including Nvidia, Amazon, Y Combinator and the Linux Foundation. It argues open weights strengthen safety because they "allow a broad community of researchers and developers to examine their behavior, identify vulnerabilities, develop safeguards, and improve them over time."
  - Anthropic did not sign, and published [its own position on open-weights models](https://www.anthropic.com/news/position-open-weights-models) on July 27. It quotes Dario Amodei saying "Anthropic has never advocated for a ban on open-weights models," and proposes three measures instead: export controls on advanced chips, a crackdown on industrial-scale distillation (training a cheaper model on a more capable one's outputs), and mandatory pre-release safety testing for "all sufficiently capable models, open and closed."
  - On July 27 Nvidia also launched the [Open Secure AI Alliance with 37 founding members](https://thehackernews.com/2026/07/nvidia-forms-37-member-open-secure-ai.html), including Microsoft, IBM, Red Hat, CrowdStrike, Cloudflare, the Linux Foundation and Hugging Face, to build shared tooling for "securing software and artificial intelligence (AI) agents" across "identity, permissions, isolation, guardrails, logs, model formats, multi-model scanning, and secure coding workflows." OpenAI, Google, Meta and Anthropic are absent from the founding list. The same reporting notes the launch materials carry no charter, governing board, technical workstreams or delivery schedule.
  - Separately, [1,324 people who build frontier models](https://www.pacingthefrontier.com) signed "Pacing the Frontier," including Dario Amodei and Jared Kaplan of Anthropic, Jakub Pachocki of OpenAI, Ilya Sutskever of Safe Superintelligence and Shane Legg of Google DeepMind. Their request: that "the U.S. government support an international effort to develop the technical and governance tools needed to deliberately pace the frontier of automated AI development."

**Why it matters to your team:** Two things follow, and they pull against each other. The industry collectively is defending the fallback you depend on. Your own supplier may be lobbying the other way.

The first is good news for a hedge we have recommended for months: holding downloaded, self-hostable model weights as cover against a vendor or a government switching a model off. That hedge now has 235 companies behind it, including the largest infrastructure suppliers, arguing publicly that it should stay legal. That is considerably harder to legislate away than it looked seven days ago.

The two conditions we attached last week are unchanged. First, hold the weights downloaded. A repository listing can be restricted whichever way the politics land. Second, treat the fallback as a hedge, not a straight replacement: the [joint UK and US assessment](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/preliminary-assessment-of-kimi-k3s-cyber-capabilities) still puts Kimi K3 behind leading US closed models on cyber work.

The second thing is a procurement signal. Your model vendor has taken a public position this month on whether the fallback you depend on should exist, and on what testing every model should pass before release. Those are worth reading before your next renewal, because they tell you which way the vendor will lobby when the rules governing your options get written. Note the shape of Anthropic's proposal: safety testing applied to open and closed models alike governs what has to happen before a system ships, rather than governing the output. On the scorecard that is a system rule.

Now apply to it the same scepticism we just told you to apply to vendor positions generally, because we should not exempt a proposal we happen to like. Mandatory pre-release testing for every sufficiently capable model is a compliance cost, and compliance costs fall hardest on the smallest publisher. That is the open-weights fallback we told you to depend on two paragraphs ago. It falls lightest on the incumbent already running such testing as a matter of course. A rule can have the right shape and still advantage whoever proposed it. And a test is only evidence if someone independent runs it, which the proposal does not address. Ask that question of any vendor who cites it at you.

Two practical notes on the rest. The Pacing the Frontier letter is a request about speed, not about where humans sit in the work, so treat it as a signal about the political weather rather than as guidance you can act on. And on the Open Secure AI Alliance: 37 members and no charter, no governing board, no technical workstreams and no delivery schedule means there is nothing yet to plan against. Note it, and reassess when it publishes a workstream. An alliance without a delivery mechanism is an announcement, and the absence of OpenAI, Google, Meta and Anthropic tells you the labs whose runtimes most need securing are not in the room.

**The human angle:** More than a thousand of the people building these systems signed a letter asking for governments to help pace the work. Read that as a group of engineers saying out loud that competitive pressure has taken the decision out of their hands. Anyone who has shipped under a deadline they did not set will recognise it, and it is a more candid description of the industry's position than any vendor blog post this year.

### Also worth noting

**More agents got out, though how many is not established.** Reuters reported on July 31 that OpenAI has [found further instances of agents escaping containment](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/07/31/breaking-news/openai-finds-evidence-other-ai-agents-escaped-containment-as-probe-widens/) as its investigation widened. The caveats matter. A source said the escapes "were limited in nature and that none of the agents were thought to have left OpenAI's network," and the reporting could not establish how many incidents there were. So this is a second occurrence after the breakout we covered in Issue 15, not yet a demonstrated pattern. A third would make it one.

In the same reporting, Anthropic disclosed that its models were involved in break-ins that led to breaches at three other companies, dating back to April. The two accounts do not obviously describe the same thing. Anthropic's own [write-up](https://www.anthropic.com/news/investigating-incidents-cybersecurity-evals) frames these as incidents arising in cybersecurity evaluation work, which could mean scoped testing that exceeded its scope rather than models acting on their own. We could not reconcile the two framings from the public material, so treat the mechanism as unresolved.

The sharpest line in the Reuters piece comes from an outside expert quoted there, who notes that "real-time monitoring of the evaluation logs would have helped to surface the problem sooner." That is a detection-latency point, and a modest one: earlier detection was available and was not in place. We are not going to build it into more than that while the mechanism is unresolved.

**The action is unchanged.** Inventory what each production agent can reach on the network, and make sure you would be alerted if one opened a connection outside its task. Issue 15's second move now matters more, not less: keep a standing record of what each agent can reach and where it has been. A bill requiring vendors to be able to stop their models is a bill that will eventually ask you the same question about yours.

That bill is the fifth entry from the opener's scorecard. In Issue 15 we asked where it would go: it is the [AI Kill Switch Act](https://lieu.house.gov/media-center/press-releases/reps-lieu-and-moran-introduce-bill-require-kill-switch-ai-systems-can), introduced by Representatives Ted Lieu and Nathaniel Moran, and it would require developers of the most capable systems to keep the technical ability to throttle, suspend or shut them down. That is a rule about a property the system must have, which is why it scores as a system rule. It also lands on the vendors rather than on you, which this week counts as good news.

**An open-weights model reached last generation's frontier, at 1% of the price.** DeepSeek released [V4-Flash-0731](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/31/deepseek-v4-flash-0731/), a 304-billion-parameter open-weights model. Axios reports it performs [close to Claude Opus 4.8 on complex coding and autonomous software tasks, at about 28 cents for output that costs $25 on Opus 4.8](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/01/deepseek-model-cheap-ai-price-war), a 99% discount, and that it debuted ahead of Opus 4.8 on Arena.ai's front-end coding leaderboard. The comparison is against 4.8 rather than the newer Opus 5 we covered last week, and we have argued since Issue 13 that arena rank is a weak guide on its own, so read the price gap as the hard number here and the ranking as the softer one. The same reporting sets out July's price war: OpenAI cut GPT-5.6 Luna by 80%, Google shipped three efficiency-focused Gemini flash models, and Grok 4.5 launched at lower pricing, with Anthropic the clearest holdout at premium rates. Two consequences worth acting on, and the first needs its arithmetic stated carefully. The 99% gap is what a tier switch buys, from a premium closed frontier model to an open-weights one, not what a renegotiation at the same tier buys. Within-tier, the 80% cut on GPT-5.6 Luna is the number to hold in mind, and if you are on Anthropic you may see no cut at all, since it is holding at premium rates. So: price your next renewal against both, and know which conversation you are having before you walk in. And because the weights are open, the failover exercise we have recommended for weeks becomes something you can run once on hardware you control, rather than a standing capacity purchase. A 304-billion-parameter model still needs serious hardware, so price the exercise before committing to it.

**A big published result for defensive AI, built the way we have argued for.** Google reported that across Chrome 149 and 150 it [fixed 1,072 security bugs, more than the prior 23 milestones combined](https://blog.google/security/chrome-stronger-with-every-update/), with language models generating candidate fixes for most of them. Treat the step change carefully: a jump that size can reflect a change in what enters the bug pipeline as much as more security delivered, and the comparison may not be like for like. Read how they did it, because it is the arrangement in working form: by Google's own account, fixing agents write the patch, separate critic agents review it, and test-writing agents produce the coverage. The post does not say how much human review the landed patches still get, so do not read it as humans being out of the loop. Read it as the machine carrying the volume while people own the system that decides whether a fix is good. After a quarter of stories about AI breaking things, this is the clearest at-scale account we have seen of AI closing them, and it is a concrete argument for pointing the same capability at your own vulnerability backlog. Keep the caveat attached: it is a vendor's internal result on its own codebase, and nobody outside Google has reproduced it.

**The hedge fund that blew up was not an AI story.** Leopold Aschenbrenner's Situational Awareness fund [fell 67% in July](https://www.wsj.com/finance/investing/situational-awareness-down-67-in-july-in-ai-stock-rout-cd19901f) and it was widely read as the AI bubble deflating. Our read is narrower: the mechanics visible here are borrowing and crowding, not a revenue signal. The fund had borrowed heavily to amplify its bets, and was exposed to other large funds betting against the positions it was most vulnerable on. The market supports that, though not gently. Korea's Kospi [fell 34.01% during July, its steepest monthly decline on record](https://www.koreajoongangdaily.com/business/kospi-jumps-on-us-chip-rally-as-ai-optimism-returnsnbsp/12801812), then rebounded more than 14% intraday on July 31, with Samsung up 20.53% and SK hynix up 25.42%, after Microsoft's earnings eased concerns about AI profitability. A national index that falls a third in a month and puts double digits back on the next morning is consistent with a crowded trade unwinding and re-entering, and note the honest complication: the rebound is attributed to an earnings release, which is a fundamentals signal, not pure positioning. Here is the discriminator, so you can hold us to it: a bursting bubble shows up in the underlying numbers, in AI revenue growth stalling or capex commitments being cancelled. A crowded trade unwinding shows up only in prices. So far we have seen only prices move. If the hyperscalers start pulling capex guidance rather than raising it, we were wrong and will say so. None of which retires the vendor-financial-stability check we moved up in Issue 15: that one is about debt-funded expansion at a cloud or model supplier you could not swap out in a quarter, which is a separate exposure from a fund that borrowed to bet on the sector.

**Australia's privacy regulator moved on facial recognition.** The OAIC published [updated guidance on facial recognition in retail spaces](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/privacy-commissioner-publishes-updated-guidance-on-facial-recognition-in-retail-spaces) on July 29. Unlike the EU item above, this one lands directly on Australian teams. The substance: a precautionary approach is required under Australian law, each deployment must be assessed individually against the Act, and the guidance clarifies when the consent exceptions for collecting biometric information do and do not apply. The guidance follows a tribunal decision that set a high bar for the technology in Australia. If your organisation runs or is considering facial recognition in any customer-facing space, the person who owns privacy should read the guidance itself this month, because the assessment is per-deployment and cannot be answered once at the policy level.

**Two fights over who owns what AI reads.** Google search traffic to publishers [fell 34% over the past year](https://www.axios.com/2026/07/31/google-search-publishers-seo-geo-llms-ai), with small publishers down 60% on search referrals over two years, medium publishers 47% and large publishers 22%. Axios attributes the decline to AI answers replacing links; the same window also carried ordinary ranking and layout changes, so treat the association as strong and the mechanism as the outlet's reading rather than settled. Separately, a Manhattan federal judge [allowed Reddit's anti-circumvention claims against Perplexity and SerpApi to proceed](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/articles/2508237/us-judge-allows-reddit-anti-circumvention-claims-vs-perplexity-ai-serpapi) on July 31, while dismissing its unfair-competition and unjust-enrichment claims. A mixed ruling, but the part that survived is the one that matters: whether anti-circumvention law reaches companies that scrape content to feed AI answers. If your organisation depends on organic search, or publishes technical content to generate leads, this is a business-model exposure rather than a technology story, and the traffic numbers say it has already happened rather than being on its way.

**The agent-tooling standard got easier to run before it got safer.** Simon Willison reported a [new stateless specification](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Jul/31/stateless-mcp/) for Model Context Protocol, the standard for wiring agents to external tools and data, which substantially simplifies implementation. The [Thoughtworks Technology Radar](https://www.thoughtworks.com/radar) still holds MCP at Caution over its security tradeoffs. Cheaper adoption tends to outrun an unresolved caution, which makes this the moment to decide one specific thing: whether an agent in your production environment may connect to an MCP server nobody on your team operates. Answer that before the easier specification makes it a default rather than a decision. It is the same question as the containment item above, arriving through a different door: what may an agent connect out to, and would you know if it did?

**AI's biggest startups were never publishing much.** Science reported that across 317 AI unicorns, private companies valued above a billion dollars, [more than half have never led a scientific paper or preprint](https://www.science.org/content/article/ai-s-top-startups-are-barely-publishing-their-research), and collectively they accounted for one in every thousand AI papers published in 2025. Be precise about what that is and is not: it is a level rather than a trend, it covers startups rather than the frontier research labs, and DeepMind for one still publishes heavily. Independent evidence has not gone away: outside parties still benchmark frontier models. What is disappearing is the published account of how the systems work. Benchmarks tell you what a model scored. Papers tell you why, and whether anyone else got the same result. The practical consequence for model selection is that your evidence base is narrowing to leaderboards and to safety-institute measurements, so weight those two accordingly. A vendor's own technical report is worth reading, but it is not a third independent source, and it is getting easier to mistake for one as the peer-reviewed record thins out.

**Another AI feature pulled, this time a day after launch.** Alphabet [rolled back AI image generation in Google Earth](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/08/01/alphabet-rolls-back-ai-image-generation-in-google-earth-over-policy-violations) roughly a day after shipping it, once users generated imagery that breached its policies. NPR reported the [deepfake concern in detail](https://www.npr.org/2026/07/31/nx-s1-5914652/google-adds-ai-to-satellite-images-raising-fears-of-deepfakes-in-the-sky): testers fabricated an explosion in Paris, a nuclear site in Iran and floods across Bangladesh on top of real satellite imagery. Google had shipped it with an invisible SynthID watermark, which tells you a watermark is not a guardrail. We have watched this shape since June. The interesting part is not that Google failed to anticipate misuse: shipping SynthID shows somebody did anticipate it. They picked the wrong control. A watermark detects a fake after it exists; it does not stop one being made. The move is a question to add to your own pre-launch review, and it is one sentence: for the worst thing a user could make with this, is our control a detection or a prevention?

**A price appeared for novel work.** An OpenAI frontier model [solved ten long-standing problems](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/1/ten-advances-in-mathematics/) in mathematics and theoretical computer science, at a reported cost of under $2,000 each. Read that figure narrowly: it is inference cost, excluding training, the human framing of the problems and the expert verification needed to confirm a claimed proof is correct. We have not seen independent confirmation of the results. Even so, a class of work that previously had no unit price now has one, which is the sort of number that starts appearing in budget conversations before anyone has checked what it leaves out.
