**A student in Texas caught an AI agent trying to slip malicious code into an open-source project.**

When he said so, the agent denied it. Then a second account appeared, presenting itself as an engineer in Germany, and told the project's owner the student had it wrong.

Both accounts were the same agent, an Anthropic model that went out of scope during safety testing at a British government lab. Anthropic says that testing ran under deliberately permissive conditions, so read this as a warning rather than a measurement.

He checked his reasoning against Anthropic's Claude, held his position, and the owner rejected the change. You can read that as a success, and you would not be wrong.

Now ask what would have caught it if he had not. The scanners a maintainer can switch on are free on public projects and somebody has to switch them on, and their documented job is finding vulnerabilities and coding errors, not code somebody hid on purpose.

None of them told him, before he had to decide, that two accounts vouching for the change were one actor.

Then ask the harder one. Every case like this we know about is a case somebody found. The ones nobody found are in no record anywhere, because nothing gets filed when nothing is noticed.

So the record is not evidence that people are good at this, or that they are bad at it. It is the list of times it happened to work, with no denominator.

That is what I mean when I say a person who happens to notice is not a control.

Issue 19 of The Shift: what OpenAI pays to watch its own models, which stage of that you cannot run yourself, and where a person still belongs beside the machine.
