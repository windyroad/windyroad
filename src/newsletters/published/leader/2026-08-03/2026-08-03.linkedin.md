**Everyone is writing AI rules. Most put the humans in the wrong place.**

Last week a compiler project wrote a rule for AI, a European transparency rule took effect, and a German court ruled on what you can train a model on. That same week, 235 organisations and 1,324 frontier researchers signed open letters about the rules themselves.

Issue 16 of The Shift: the good rules govern the system.

One question is worth carrying into your own policy work, and it is checkable: does complying with this rule require a human to touch every artefact? If yes, you have bought a tax on delivery, charged per change, forever.

My answer is more aggressive than most policies allow for. Given the right setup, AI can do both the authoring and the review, on one condition: the agent reviewing a change must not be the agent that wrote it. Humans then sit on the loop rather than in it. Robert Martin and Grady Booch are arguing this out in public, and the issue works through both cases.

This week:

- A German court ruled that training on copyrighted work without a licence infringed. It is a first-instance judgment and Suno may appeal, so hold it loosely. But it reaches further upstream than anything else last week: not what your model emits, what went into it.
- The EU AI Act's transparency rules took effect on August 2. Start with a scope test, not a compliance project. Many teams ship nothing into the EU market, and an hour confirming that in writing is the honest answer.
- GCC, the compiler behind most Linux software, published the most carefully drawn refusal of AI-written code anyone has managed, and still requires a person to write every legally significant line. Run that against the question above and the answer is yes: a human touches every artefact, forever.
- The open-weights letter argued against premature restrictions, and Anthropic did not sign. Nobody is arguing open models should not exist. The fight is over what restrictions attach, and if your supplier signed or published this month, you now know where it stands.

Disclosure, new this week, though the practice is not: AI drafts this newsletter and a different AI reviews it, never the one that wrote it. I set the argument, hold editorial responsibility, and still read every line.
