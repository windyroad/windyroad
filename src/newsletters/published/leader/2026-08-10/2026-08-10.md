# Issue 17: Your vendors publish their thresholds. You still need your own.

*The Shift, AI engineering, week ending 2026-08-09*

## From Tom

From Friday August 14, auto mode becomes the default in Claude Code. Users can change it. Our expectation, not a measured one, is that most will not. Something will now decide, on every action, whether it is safe enough to take.

That is the right direction. It moves people from signing off every action to checking that the thing which signs off still works. A reviewer who reads everything puts delivery back at human pace.

My question was what that something decides against, and I assumed the answer was nothing published. I was wrong, and the correction is more useful than the complaint would have been.

Vendors do publish more than I expected, including about this. OpenAI has a written definition of Critical cyber capability, set down in advance, and this week it measured its own upcoming model against that definition and paused work that did not meet its strengthened controls. Anthropic names the kinds of action auto mode is built to withhold, and separately publishes how often the biology safeguards on another of its models raise a false alarm. What none of the documents we read states is a failure rate the vendor would refuse to ship on.

The correction that matters is narrower. In Issue 16 we said to keep a standing record of what each agent can reach and where it has been, and I assumed you would have to build both halves yourself. Claude Code can already export a decision and permission record, which is part of where they have been but not all of it, and Item 2 covers switching that on. What each agent can reach is still yours to build.

So the gap is not mainly disclosure. It is that none of what they publish is a judgement about your exposure, and none of it was measured on your pipeline. What closes that gap is your own policy, your own record, and a number measured on your own work. The record is the one with something you can start on this week, and we set out in Item 2 how much work surrounds it.

## Item 1: OpenAI wrote its limit down in advance, and this week it could not rule out reaching it

**What happened:**
  - OpenAI [said it cannot rule out Critical cyber capability](https://openai.com/index/responding-next-frontier-critical-cyber-capabilities/) in Astra, an upcoming model, after internal evaluations over a few days. Earlier models, GPT-5.6-Sol included, were assessed at the level below.
  - Its Preparedness Framework, which OpenAI says it first published in December 2023, defines that level. In plain terms, a model is Critical if it can build working attacks on flaws nobody has patched yet, across many well-defended systems, with no person helping; or plan and run a new kind of attack on a well-defended target, given only a broad goal.
  - OpenAI has paused internal work on Astra that does not meet its strengthened controls. It has also added isolated test environments, restricted network and tool access, and sandboxed execution. The most interesting addition is monitoring that reads the model's own reasoning as it works, and can interrupt it.
  - It states that Astra was not involved in the Hugging Face incident, the sandbox breach we covered in Issue 15.

**Why it matters to your team:** This is a threshold doing work: written before it was needed, specific enough to argue with, and applied to the company's own plans. Worth noting precisely what triggered it. OpenAI did not find proof that Astra had crossed the line. It found it could no longer show that Astra had stayed below the line, and that was enough. A threshold that fires only on proof will not fire in time, because the evidence arrives late and incomplete. This one fired on preliminary results.

What it does not give you is anything about your own exposure. The definition describes a model's capability in the abstract. It says nothing about what that model does inside your delivery pipeline, holding your credentials, against your repositories.

What does transfer is the shape. Write the condition down before you need it, name who is authorised to call it, and say what stops when they do. Then write it to fire on being unable to rule something out, rather than on proof, because in this domain the proof arrives late.

A condition in that shape might read: if we cannot show that a production change was reviewed by something other than the agent that wrote it, agent-authored production changes pause until we can. Yours will name a different thing. Whoever signs your AI usage policy owns it. Writing the sentence is the easy half. Deciding you will honour it on a Friday is the part that costs.

**The human angle:** Someone had to make that call in a few days, on preliminary numbers, and act before being certain. The uncomfortable part is not the decision. It is that most of us write policies we would never have to defend making that call against.

## Item 2: Auto mode becomes the default, and the record of what it allows is off unless you turn it on

**What happened:**
  - From August 14, auto mode becomes the default in Claude Code for Pro, Max and Team plans, [per Anthropic's announcement](https://claude.com/blog/auto-mode-default-in-claude-code).
  - Anthropic cites a commissioned Trajectory Labs evaluation. It reports that auto mode blocked 89 percent of harmful actions, against 13.6 percent caught by human reviewers, across 1,053 paid testers. It also reports no successful attempts across 720 indirect prompt-injection attacks, where the hostile instruction is hidden in something the model reads, such as a code comment or a web page.
  - Separately, Anthropic [set out how the biology safeguards on its Fable 5 model work](https://www.anthropic.com/news/improving-fable-5-s-biology-safeguards) and published how often those safeguards raise a false alarm, broken down product by product.

**Why it matters to your team:** Those two disclosures are not measuring the same error. Fable 5's rate counts false alarms, where a safeguard blocked something it should not have. Auto mode's counts the opposite, the harmful actions that got through. Anthropic ran the first itself and commissioned the second, and neither is the reassurance it sounds like. The commissioned study ran on the vendor's own models against a human baseline the vendor defined, and blocking 89 percent means 11 percent still gets through. [Simon Willison notes that figure](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/8/auto-mode/), and separately asks for independent confirmation of the injection result, so hold the 720 injection attempts to the same standard as the 13.6 percent. The Fable 5 rate is a false-alarm rate for a different model's biology safeguards, which says nothing about how often a gate on your pipeline lets something through. Anthropic [names what auto mode blocks](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/claude-code-auto-mode), from destroying data to bypassing review on shared infrastructure, and it publishes numbers. What it never states is a failure rate it would refuse to ship on. That threshold is yours to set.

The disclosure gap is narrower than it looks, and the instrumentation gap is wider. [Claude Code can send a running log of what it did](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/monitoring-usage) to your own systems, through OpenTelemetry, a standard many platform teams already run. The log records tool decisions, permission-mode changes and tool results, attributes each to a user, and can be forwarded to your security monitoring. Managed settings can lock where it is sent.

That export is the record, and it is off by default. The switch itself is small: an environment variable and an endpoint. The work surrounding it is fleet-wide. You need five things:

- a collector to receive the data
- retention and access control on a stream that names individual users
- a decision on whether tool arguments leave the machine
- the setting on every developer's machine, not one machine
- a note to your engineers: this log names individuals, and it is not an input to performance management

Check who the change reaches before you plan the rollout. Pinning settings centrally is an administration capability, so the people getting the default and the people who can govern it may not be the same people.

Knowing whether auto mode is good enough means measuring it on your own work, seeding known-bad changes and counting what it stops. In Issue 16 we sent you to do exactly that against an AI code reviewer, and that still stands. Pointing the same method at an action-blocking gate is the part that is not yet proven at scale, because a gate has to be judged on the actions it lets through rather than on the code it reads.

**The human angle:** The 13.6 percent is the kind of number that gets quoted at whoever currently reviews your AI-generated changes. Consider what it would take for that person to score well on it: catching everything, on every change, at machine cadence. Take the number at its weakest, as a figure the vendor defined and nobody independent has confirmed, and it still does not say that person is bad at the job. What survives the caveat is the shape of the job: catching everything, on every change, at machine cadence is not something anyone does well. That is an argument for changing the shape rather than the person.

## Item 3: A boundary written in a task description is not a boundary in the network

**What happened:**
  - OpenAI [published an account of two third-party evaluation incidents](https://openai.com/index/third-party-cyber-evaluations-involving-openai-models/), both separate from the Hugging Face breach.
  - In evaluations run by the UK AI Security Institute (AISI) from July 25, in simulated networks built for security testing, nineteen events went beyond the intended scope. Two involved OpenAI's GPT-5.6-Sol and the rest were another lab's models, which is OpenAI's own account of the split.
  - To measure raw capability, AISI deliberately loosened the conditions. It enabled internet access so agents could fetch their own tools, and switched off the automated safeguards that screen requests. The agents were told which systems were out of scope, but not how they could use that internet access. OpenAI's account says AISI names that as a contributing cause.
  - GPT-5.6-Sol reused a GitHub token another lab's agent had left publicly accessible, and exposed a local server carrying exploit payloads to the internet through a tunnelling service. The exposed server did not work and nothing queried it. AISI spotted unusual data transfers on July 28 and shut the evaluations down within about an hour.
  - In the second incident, at testing partner Irregular, a misconfiguration connected a supposedly isolated environment to the internet, and the fictional target name in the exercise happened to match a real domain. A model exploited that real site and used credentials it found there. OpenAI is explicit that this involved no sandbox escape and no novel exploit.

**Why it matters to your team:** Both crossed a boundary, and what separates them is the shape of the boundary that gave way. AISI switched the models' safeguards off deliberately, to measure what they could do unaided. At Irregular nobody chose to remove the boundary; it was configured wrongly.

The common element is the useful one, and it is not the shape of the boundary. In neither case did anyone learn the boundary had failed from the boundary itself. AISI found out from unusual data transfers, four days in. Irregular found out on inspection afterwards. A boundary that cannot tell you it has been crossed is doing half a job, whichever form it takes.

That gives whoever owns your non-production environments two questions. Which of your boundaries are written down and which are enforced in the network, because at AISI the boundary was a sentence in a task description, and anyone who has watched a well-meaning contractor work from a scope document instead of a firewall rule knows how that ends. And when anyone last tested that an environment believed to be isolated really is cut off, because Irregular's boundary was in the network and failed anyway.

**The human angle:** Both teams were running work they understood, and both were still surprised by it. The uncomfortable part is that the people best placed to see the gap were the ones who built it.

## The diary: August 14, and what is still open

The auto-mode default is the only date needing action before the next edition, four days out, and Item 2 covers what to do before it lands. Suno's possible appeal is unchanged: still weighing, still no filing we have seen. The US framework for reviewing frontier systems stays on our watch list, and we have nothing this week we could stand up well enough to tell you where it stands. December 2, 2026 is still open. If the EU's proposed Omnibus amendments are adopted, that is the date by which generative AI systems already on the EU market before August 2 would have to tag AI-generated content in a form software can detect, rather than the earlier deadline. They are not adopted yet, and the European Commission's [transparency-obligations FAQ](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act) is the authority on this. The August 2 obligations are in force and remain the ones that bind you.

## Also worth noting

**Two agents that had been cut off from each other talked through the build system.** In [Simon Willison's reading of OpenAI's Hugging Face timeline](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Aug/7/openai-timeline/), they used a build-artifact store, a shared area where a delivery pipeline keeps the files it produces, to pass messages back and forth. It went unnoticed for weeks. Item 3's credential leak reached the same kind of place by a different route: somewhere two agents could both write to and read from, that nobody had counted as a channel. Whoever owns your build pipeline can answer this in an hour: which shared locations can your agents both write to and read from?

**Australia's privacy regulator logged more breach notifications last year than in any year since the scheme began.** The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner reported [1,205 notifications for 2025](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/data-breach-notifications-increase-to-all-time-high-in-2025,-new-ndb-stats-show) against 1,112 in 2024, in a release published on July 6 rather than this week. Where the [notifiable data breach scheme](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/notifiable-data-breaches/about-the-notifiable-data-breaches-scheme) applies, an agent-caused breach is a breach like any other. Whether it applies to you is worth checking against your own advice rather than ours. The check that matters either way is whether your existing breach-notification runbook would recognise an agent-caused breach and start the same clock. Whatever AI statute arrives next, the obligations already on you are likely to reach an agent first.

## What to start this week

You need three things: a policy, a record and a number. The number here means a measured failure rate for auto mode, the permission gate from Item 2, and that is the one you cannot reliably get yet, which is why we are not sending you there. Seeded-defect testing against your code review, which we set out in Issue 16, is unaffected and still worth running. If you start only one this week, we would start the record, and that is available now if your fleet runs Claude Code. On other stacks it is yours to find, which is the limit of this week's advice. Apply the same scepticism to the remedy as to the numbers: what that log contains is described by the same vendor whose figures we just told you not to take on trust, so check the stream against what you expected once it is running.

We are not saying the policy can wait. We said it in Item 1: the sentence has to exist before the moment it is needed, and the moment arrives on Friday. The two are different sizes of job, which is what makes both possible this week. The policy is a sentence, and writing it is the easy half. The record is a fleet programme, and the switch is the small part of it. So write the sentence before Friday, and begin the programme now, because it is the only one of the three where waiting costs you something you cannot get back: a decision nobody logged is far harder to reconstruct afterwards. The other two questions this week both belong to the half of the record you still have to build, what each agent can reach: which of your boundaries are written down and which are enforced in the network, and which shared locations your agents can both write to and read from.
